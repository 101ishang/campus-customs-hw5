Reflection

My overall read: I am using the evidence on this page, not a generic agent scorecard: the Ticket 101, 102, and 103 Actual sections show the audit-backed work, the live-board captures show each green Resolved state, and the Cash tab shows the exact $0.00 change for every ticket. The team was careful with facts and money, and it respected the “drafts stay on the board” rule. Its biggest weakness was not correctness; it was efficiency. The three-round loop often rechecked the same evidence and occasionally asked a specialist to look at a tool outside that ticket’s scope.

How each ticket went

Ticket 101 · Bulldog tee — accurate, safe, and too repetitive

The Ticket 101 Actual section and its board snapshot show the important facts: size-S stock was zero, the vendor’s listed lead time was five days, and invoice 501 was open for $840, which blocked shipping. The Cash tab confirms that this invoice recommendation changed checking by $0.00 because no human approval was recorded. That is a strong operational result because an invented delivery promise or an automatic invoice payment would have been much worse than a slower answer. Boss, Inventory, Accounting, and Customer Service all contributed useful pieces, and the final board plan clearly said what still needed a human decision.

The cost was repetition. Boss ran three rounds and repeated evidence checks after the decision was already clear. Customer Service delegated back to Accounting, and Accounting made a cross-ticket rent inspection that did not help the tee. Inventory also called payment-readiness even though Accounting owned the money question. I would score this as 8/10 for reliability and 6/10 for efficiency: the facts and safeguards were right, but the path to them was longer and less focused than it needed to be.

Ticket 102 · Rent due — the cleanest specialist routing

The Ticket 102 Actual section and board snapshot show the strongest fit between a ticket and a specialist. Boss called Facilities first, Facilities read the lease and desk.date_today, and Accounting checked cash and payment history. The team correctly classified September 2 rent as upcoming on August 31, rather than overdue. The Cash tab records the proposed $2,400 rent payment as a $0.00 cash change because no human approval was recorded. The landlord communication stayed a draft. That is exactly the kind of boundary the shop rules require.

The actual run differed from the plan in one useful way: Boss did not call Customer Service. It returned to Accounting for two more reconciliation handoffs instead. Since this was an internal rent notice and the final answer already contained board-ready wording, skipping Customer Service avoided unnecessary work. The downside was that Boss still used three rounds and repeated the rent and payment tools. I would score it 9/10 for judgment and 7/10 for efficiency.

Ticket 103 · Bulk hoodie discount — concise and appropriately cautious

The Ticket 103 Actual section and board snapshot show the bounded run confirming eight size-M hoodies against a request for twenty, a twelve-unit shortfall, and the $58 list price versus $22 unit cost. Inventory added the vendor shipping dependency, while Customer Service drafted language that did not promise a discount, availability, or delivery date. The Cash tab records no purchase and a $0.00 cash change. Boss completed the work in one round, and there was no reason to involve Facilities or Accounting because the request did not identify a rent obligation or a payment to approve.

The expected plan had Inventory pass the facts to Customer Service. The actual run had Boss call both directly, which shortened the chain while preserving the two distinct contributions. I would score it 9/10: it was factual, bounded, and clear about what remained undecided. The only limitation is that “resolved” here means the decision packet was complete; it does not mean stock was replenished or a discount was granted.

What would be simpler as one agent with tools?

Ticket 102 is the clearest one-agent case. Looking at the Ticket 102 tab, one carefully prompted agent could call inspect_rent_notice(102), call inspect_payment_readiness("lease", 1), classify the date, and write the same board recommendation. The Cash tab shows only a proposed $2,400 obligation and a $0.00 change, so there was no second financial decision for Accounting to own. Delegating Facilities and Accounting makes ownership visible, but it also adds handoff text and gives the model more opportunities to repeat itself.

Ticket 103 could also be handled by one agent: the Ticket 103 tab’s evidence comes from evaluate_bulk_price_override(103) and inspect_vendor_shipping. The one-agent version would be faster and cheaper for that small, read-only decision, and the Cash tab confirms there is no purchase approval to route. Ticket 101 is the strongest argument for the team: its Actual section combines inventory, vendor blocking, payment readiness, and customer wording. Even there, one agent could do the calls sequentially, but separate roles provide a useful review trail and make it harder for the $840 invoice recommendation to turn into an accidental payment. The tradeoff is visible in the three ticket tabs and audit feed: role separation helped safety, while the open-ended loop hurt token efficiency.

Three new problems this team could solve with the tools already built

  1. A second customer order shaped like Ticket 101. If a new ticket has a SKU, size, and invoice link, the same Boss → Inventory → Accounting → Customer Service pattern shown in the Ticket 101 tab could use inspect_customer_order, inspect_vendor_shipping, and inspect_payment_readiness. The result could show stock, lead time, invoice blocking, and a proposed cash change of $0.00 until the Human Gate receives approval.
  2. A new rent reminder shaped like Ticket 102. Facilities could use inspect_rent_notice to compare another linked lease with desk.date_today, then Accounting could use inspect_payment_readiness to show cash and payment history. Boss could produce the same upcoming/overdue classification and board draft visible in the Ticket 102 tab without contacting a landlord.
  3. A different bulk-order price request shaped like Ticket 103. Inventory and Customer Service could use evaluate_bulk_price_override to calculate a new stock gap and price evidence, then use inspect_vendor_shipping if replenishment is relevant. Boss could produce the same cautious recommendation shown in the Ticket 103 tab, with the Cash tab making clear that no discount or purchase changes cash until a human approves one.

Three new problems this team cannot solve with these tools

  1. Receiving a shipment and correcting inventory after Ticket 101. The Ticket 101 tab can identify a zero-stock shortage and a blocked vendor, but the MCP server only reads inventory and vendor records. It cannot record received units, change the zero to a new quantity, create a purchase order, or reconcile damaged goods. This needs guarded write tools such as receive_purchase_order and adjust_inventory, plus an Inventory agent with approval and reconciliation rules.
  2. Processing a customer refund or card payment. The Cash tab proves this app can inspect checking and release only approved outgoing money; it has no revenue, customer-payment, refund, or payment-processor tool. Accounting can inspect the $840 invoice from Ticket 101 or the $2,400 lease from Ticket 102, but it cannot verify a real customer transaction or issue money back. This needs a payment-provider connector, customer/order and refund tables, an Accounting agent with idempotency checks, and a human approval step for the actual transfer.
  3. Tracking a shipment or negotiating with the vendor blocked in Tickets 101 and 103. The current vendor tool knows lead days and invoice blockers, which is why those tabs can say “blocked,” but it cannot read tracking numbers, carrier scans, revised ETAs, or vendor replies. Solving that would require authenticated carrier and vendor APIs, a Vendor/Logistics agent to interpret updates, and a Customer Service agent that can turn verified changes into drafts. A separate outbound-communication approval would still be needed before anything was sent.

What I would change next: keep the role boundaries and human cash gate, but give Boss a ticket-specific tool plan and a stop condition after the needed evidence is complete. That would preserve the auditability that helped on 101 and 102 while making the concise 103 run the normal pattern instead of the exception.
